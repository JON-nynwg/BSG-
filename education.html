THE BASICS

What is epilepsy?

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological condition marked by a tendency to have recurrent, unprovoked seizures — brief disruptions caused by unusual electrical activity in the brain. It affects people of every age, background and country, and it is one of the most common neurological conditions in the world.

Focal Seizures

Begin in one specific area of the brain. They may affect movement, sensation or awareness, and can sometimes spread to involve the whole brain.

Generalized Seizures

Involve both sides of the brain from the onset — ranging from brief lapses in awareness to full-body convulsions.

Every Case Is Different

Seizure type, frequency and triggers vary widely from person to person, which is why individualized care and diagnosis matter.

BE PREPARED

Seizure first aid: what to do.

Most seizures are not medical emergencies, but knowing how to respond keeps the person safe and reduces fear for everyone involved.

Stay calm and time it

Note when the seizure starts. Most last only one to three minutes.

Protect from injury

Clear the area of anything hard or sharp. Cushion their head if you can.

Ease them onto their side

This helps keep the airway clear and lets any fluid drain safely from the mouth.

Never restrain them or put anything in their mouth

You cannot stop a seizure by holding someone down, and objects in the mouth can cause injury.

Stay with them until it passes

Speak calmly and reassure them as awareness returns — they may feel confused or tired afterward.

Call for emergency medical help if:

The seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, another starts right after, the person is injured, pregnant, or doesn't regain consciousness — or if it's their first known seizure.

END THE STIGMA

Myths vs. facts.

Misinformation is one of the biggest barriers people with epilepsy face. Here's what the evidence actually says.

Myth

You should put something in the mouth of someone having a seizure.

Fact

Never put anything in their mouth — it can cause injury and doesn't prevent tongue-biting.

Myth

Epilepsy is contagious.

Fact

Epilepsy cannot be spread from person to person in any way.

Myth

People with epilepsy can't live normal lives.

Fact

With proper treatment, most people with epilepsy work, study, marry and raise families like anyone else.

Myth

Epilepsy is caused by curses or evil spirits.

Fact

Epilepsy is a neurological condition caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain — not a spiritual affliction.

Myth

All seizures look the same.

Fact

Seizures vary widely — from brief staring spells to full-body convulsions.

Myth

Epilepsy always requires lifelong medication.

Fact

Many people become seizure-free, and some can eventually reduce or stop medication under medical supervision.
