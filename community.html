Frequently Asked Questions

What is epilepsy?

Epilepsy is a neurological condition marked by repeated, unpredictable seizures that result from unusual electrical activity in the brain. Seizures can differ widely in how severe they are, how long they last, and what symptoms they produce.

What is a seizure?

A seizure happens when there's a sudden burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. It can briefly change how someone moves, behaves, feels, or how aware they are of their surroundings. Seizures take many forms depending on where in the brain they start and how far they spread, and most run their course within a couple of minutes.

How can I support someone living with epilepsy?

The best support starts with understanding — learning the facts helps push back against myths and stigma. Encourage them to stick with their treatment plan and attend medical appointments, and offer steady emotional support rather than treating them differently because of their diagnosis. Helping someone feel accepted goes a long way toward a full, stigma-free life.

How is epilepsy treated?

Treatment is tailored to the person and usually centers on anti-seizure medication to prevent or reduce seizures. Lifestyle habits — enough sleep, lower stress, avoiding known triggers — also play a role. For some, dietary approaches like the ketogenic diet or, in select cases, surgery may be considered.

Can epilepsy be cured?

Epilepsy isn't always something that can be fully cured, but it can very often be well managed. Many people become seizure-free with the right treatment, and others see a significant drop in how often seizures occur.

How is epilepsy diagnosed?

Diagnosis usually combines a detailed medical history, a description of what happens during a seizure, a neurological exam, and tests like an EEG to track brain activity. Imaging such as an MRI or CT scan may also be used to check for any structural cause.

What should I do if I witness a seizure?

Stay calm, and clear the area of anything that could cause injury. Gently guide the person away from hazards, ease them onto their side to keep their airway clear, and never put anything in their mouth. Stay with them until it passes, and call for medical help if it lasts more than five minutes or they're hurt.

What causes epilepsy?

Epilepsy can stem from many sources — a brain injury from trauma or a stroke, genetics, a brain tumor, infections such as meningitis, developmental conditions, or certain metabolic disorders.

Can someone with epilepsy live a normal life?

Yes — with proper treatment and management, most people with epilepsy go on to lead full, active lives, including school, work, sports and relationships. Support from family, friends and healthcare providers makes a real difference.

Are there different types of epilepsy?

Yes. Epilepsy is classified by where in the brain the seizures start and how they present — common categories include focal seizures, which begin in one area of the brain, and generalized seizures, which involve both sides at once.

Is epilepsy contagious?

No. Epilepsy cannot be spread from person to person through contact, air or any other means — it is a neurological condition, not an infection.

Do flashing lights trigger seizures in everyone with epilepsy?

No. Only a small share of people with epilepsy are photosensitive, meaning flashing or strobing lights can trigger a seizure for them specifically — it isn't a universal trigger.
